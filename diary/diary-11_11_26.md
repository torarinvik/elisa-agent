# Research diary, 11 November 2026

## Teaching by takeover: design and preregistration

This fixes the design and the predictions before any theory, code or measurement exists for it.

### Aim

An agent acts in an environment. A teacher watches, takes over when the agent goes wrong, demonstrates, and hands back.
The quantity to minimize is the teacher's effort: actions demonstrated and takeovers made before the skill passes the
gate. The first tests use a scripted teacher in small symbolic environments. A person, a record key and raw screens come
later and are outside this entry.

### Parts, and the earlier path each one takes up

1. **A skill is a machine that emits an action at each step.** It reads observations and its state fixes the next
   action. This is the sequence-output class that the reasoning tasks also lack (cipher, dedupe), so one learner serves
   both.
2. **A takeover is a counterexample.** The table learner of chapter 51 conjectures a machine and repairs it from a
   sequence it gets wrong. Here the sequence is the episode up to the takeover, and the demonstration that follows labels
   every prefix of the rest of that episode.
3. **The agent asks only what it can reach.** A query is an episode the agent can steer the environment into, answered
   by the teacher's next action. The learner may not ask about arbitrary sequences.
4. **The agent hands control back itself.** A learned machine refuses an observation it never saw. A refusal becomes a
   request for a demonstration, so the teacher need not watch every step.
5. **Admission is unchanged.** An episode is correct if the teacher would not have taken over. The gate draws fresh
   starting conditions and keeps its guarantee (tolerance 5%, confidence 0.001).
6. **A certified skill becomes an expert.** Later skills may call it as one action. This is the first test of
   compounding on anything learned from queries.
7. **Evidence.** Two seeds from the start. Half the environments are sealed before the first run and opened once.

Left out for now: learning from cases without a teacher, perception, a learned proposer, and allocation between
learners, whose ceiling was one task at the default budget.

### Result wanted on paper first

Each takeover exposes a pair of histories the conjecture merged and the teacher separates, so it should add at least one
state. With Theorem 51.1 that gives: a skill with a machine of `n` states needs at most `n - 1` takeovers, however long
the episodes are. The effort of teaching is then bounded by the size of the skill and not by the time spent playing. To
be stated and proved in Lean before implementation. If it fails as stated, the failure is recorded and the design is
reviewed before any code.

### Experiments, in order

| Stage | Question | Teacher |
|---|---|---|
| A | Does the takeover count stay within the bound, and how many demonstrated actions reach the gate? | Scripted, takes over at the first wrong action |
| B | Does an installed skill cut the effort for a skill built on it? | As A |
| C | What does a late takeover cost? | Takes over `k` steps after the wrong action, `k` in 1, 2, 4 |
| D | What does an inconsistent teacher cost? | Picks among equally good actions at random |

Baseline for A: the same learner given the same number of demonstrated actions as whole episodes recorded up front, with
no takeovers. Environments are small, deterministic and symbolic, with skills of 2 to 30 states, in a new package
outside the core. The core gains only a general interface for an environment and for a teacher.

### Predictions

- A: takeovers never exceed `n - 1` on any skill. Demonstrated actions to pass the gate are at most a third of what the
  up-front baseline needs, on at least 80% of skills. Every admitted skill completes at least 122 of 128 audit episodes.
- B: with its parts installed, a composed skill needs at most half the demonstrated actions it needs without them.
- C: effort grows with `k` and stays within four times stage A at `k = 4`. I expect the bound on takeovers to break
  here, because the wrong step is no longer the last one before the takeover.
- D: the learner as it stands fails to pass the gate on most skills. This stage measures the gap that learning from
  inconsistent cases has to close and is not expected to succeed.

Keep the design if A holds on both seeds. B, C and D decide what is built next and do not decide whether to keep it.

## Teaching by takeover: the result on paper

Proved in Lean (`NCA/Planning/Takeovers.lean`, chapter 52). No code and no measurement yet.

The bound of `n - 1` takeovers holds, with a condition I did not state in the design. Each takeover adds a state only if
the learner may then ask the teacher about spliced histories: the start of one episode followed by the end of another.
That contradicts part 3 of the design, where the agent asks only what it can reach. Where observations depend on the
agent's actions, a spliced history may never occur.

For a teacher who answers only on histories that occur, a counting bound holds instead: at most
`n (|A| log₂ n + log₂ |O|)` takeovers for a learner acting by the majority of the machines still consistent. It is
independent of episode length, like the first, and larger by about `|A| log₂ n`. As stated it needs compute exponential
in `n`.

| Skill | States `n` | Observations | Actions | Hypothetical teacher | Reachable teacher |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Small | 4 | 4 | 4 | 3 | 40 |
| Medium | 10 | 8 | 4 | 9 | 286 |
| Large | 30 | 8 | 4 | 29 | 1,238 |

### Change to the preregistration, made before any measurement

Stage A splits in two, each against the same up-front baseline.

- A1, hypothetical teacher: the scripted teacher answers spliced histories. Prediction unchanged: takeovers never exceed
  `n - 1`, and demonstrated actions plus spliced questions are at most a third of the baseline on 80% of skills.
- A2, reachable teacher: the learner merges states on the histories that occurred and asks nothing else. Prediction:
  takeovers stay within the counting bound on every skill and within `4n` on at least half; demonstrated actions are at
  most two thirds of the baseline on at least half the skills. I am less sure of A2 than of A1.

Keep the design if A1 holds on both seeds. A2 decides whether a person can be the teacher without answering questions
about situations that did not happen, and so what must be solved before a human loop.

## Counter learner without an empty answer: preregistration

Found while looking for a sign of hopeless tasks. Where a task has no answer on the empty sequence, the counter learner
can never return anything: every rebuilt answer starts from that missing value (51.5). It still asks until its limit. On
the 60 development reasoning tasks this holds for 17 of the 45 it attempts (next_term, analogy, majority, order_stat and
two boolean rules), and it reached 8,000 queries on 8 of them.

Change: the counter learner asks for the empty sequence first and declines if there is no answer. This loses nothing it
could have served.

Predictions, table mode, tasks served of 60 (now 3, 21 and 25): 3–6 at 7,500 queries, 21–24 at 27,000, 25–28 at 54,000.
Portfolio mode stays at 25 and falls from 158,069 queries to under 90,000. Keep if no budget serves fewer than now and
every served task audits at 122/128 or better.

## Teaching by takeover, stages A1 and A2: setup

Fixed before the run, in a throwaway script outside the repository. A skill is a random minimal machine with 2, 3, 4, 6,
8, 12, 16, 20, 24 or 30 states over 4 observations and 3 actions. An environment has 20 states, each showing one
observation; the next state depends on the action and on one of three chance outcomes drawn per step, and a starting
condition is the start state with its chance outcomes. Episodes last 16 steps. The teacher corrects the first wrong or
refused action before it takes effect and plays the rest of the episode; those actions are the demonstrated ones. The
gate is 135 clean fresh episodes in a row and restarts at each takeover, which is weaker than the system's gate; the
audit is 128 further episodes. A1 keeps an observation table and finds the suffix of Theorem 52.1 by bisection. A2 merges
states on the takeover episodes alone. The baseline merges states on whole episodes recorded up front and reports the
fewest that pass the same gate with no takeover, up to 2,048. Seeds 1 and 2 are run; seeds 3 and 4 are reserved unrun.

A1 stays far inside its bound: at most 7 takeovers for 30 states, because closing the table finds most states without a
takeover. Its cost is questions, about 20 to 27 per state. A2 needs about 5 to 10 takeovers per state where I predicted 4
or fewer, and 3 to 4 times the effort of A1; on seed 1 it learned 37 states for a 30-state skill and still passed. Against
recording up front, A2 needs a half to a twelfth of the demonstrated actions on skills of 8 states or more.

Limits. The skills are random machines and the teacher is consistent and immediate. The wrong action is corrected before
it takes effect, so the agent never leaves the teacher's path; a teacher who reacts afterwards is stage C. The teacher
also watched 135 to 1,004 episodes per skill, which is not counted as effort. Nothing here is in the core, and the
reserved seeds are unrun.

## Integrated takeover learner: preregistration

The loop moves into the core and the skills into a package of their own, with the same generator and seeds 1 and 2. Three
things differ from the throwaway run. Admission is the system's gate: after the loop stops at 135 clean episodes, 135
further fresh episodes must all be right, once, with no second attempt. The teacher has no action before the first
observation, so the learned machine keeps a start state of its own and the bound for the answering teacher becomes `n`
takeovers. And the answering learner also keeps what it saw in clean episodes, which costs nothing.

Predictions, 20 skills: all admitted with the answering teacher and at least 18 with demonstrations only; every admitted
skill at 122/128 or better on audit; takeovers at most `n` for the answering teacher; effort within a quarter of the
throwaway figures on at least 16 skills in each arm. Keep if the answering teacher's predictions hold.

The keep rule asked for all of the answering teacher's predictions and one of them missed, so as written it is not met.

reading and flag it as a judgement outside the rule I wrote.

The demonstration arm shows what the gate is for. Four skills (6, 24 and 30 states on seed 1, 20 on seed 2) ran 135 clean
episodes in a row, went to the gate, and failed it within 32 to 128 fresh episodes. They stay unserved, since the gate is
taken once. A run of clean episodes that restarts at every takeover is not admission, and the throwaway run's figure of 20
of 20 for this arm overstated it.

## When to stop teaching and go to the gate: preregistration

Demonstrations only, the 20 development skills. Four skills now fail the gate after 135 clean episodes: the gate admits on
zero errors in 135 fresh episodes, and a machine that is wrong on about one episode in a hundred usually fails that. Two
remedies are compared in a throwaway script. One teaches longer before the gate. The other keeps the gate's tolerance (5%)
and confidence (0.001) but uses its fixed-size form, which draws more fresh episodes and allows a certified number of
errors (20.22): 4 in 300, or 7 in 400.

| Rule | Clean run before the gate | Gate | Predicted admitted | Other prediction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Now | 135 | 135, no error | 16 (measured) | |
| Longer | 540 | 135, no error | 18–20 | watched episodes 3 to 4 times now |
| Wider | 135 | 300, up to 4 errors | 19–20 | same demonstrated actions as now |
| Widest | 135 | 400, up to 7 errors | 20 | same demonstrated actions as now |
| Early | 45 | 400, up to 7 errors | 17–20 | demonstrated actions 10–25% below now |

Adopt the rule that admits most, provided every admitted skill audits at 122/128 or better and demonstrated actions do not
exceed now by more than a tenth; on a tie, the one with fewest episodes watched in teaching and gate together. Five rules
are compared on the same 20 skills, so the winner's figure is optimistic; the reserved seeds are the check.

Wider is adopted by the rule: it admits all 20 with the fewest episodes watched among the rules that do. The harness now
uses it for both taught arms; rerun through it, the answering teacher is admitted on 20 of 20 at 128/128 and
demonstrations on 20 of 20 at 126/128 or better, with effort unchanged.

Stopping early does not pay. Demonstrated actions fall by 2%, and four skills are refused whose audits run from 119 to 125
of 128, so the gate was right to refuse at least the one below 122. Teaching longer helps less than widening the gate and
costs more watching. The guarantee is the same in every row; what changed is that the gate no longer demands a machine with
no error at all. The figure of 20 is the best of five rules on the same skills and is not yet checked on the reserved
seeds.

## Stage B, building on installed skills: preregistration

Throwaway script, demonstrations only, gate of 300 fresh episodes with up to 4 errors, audit of 128.

A composed skill has a small controller of 2 or 3 states that reads the observations. Each controller state either copies
the action of one of two part skills at that moment or takes a fixed action, and both parts are used. The parts are the
development skills of 4, 6, 8 or 12 states, one from each seed, each following the whole episode from its start. The
composed skill gets a new environment. Two draws of each of the 8 combinations give 16 composed skills.

Two arms. Without parts, the learner sees the observations alone; the smallest machine for the composed skill then has to
follow both parts and the controller at once, up to their product in states. With parts installed, the machines already
taught for the two parts (by demonstration, admitted, and not perfect) run beside the learner, and their current actions
are appended to each observation; the composed skill is then a controller-sized machine over a larger set of symbols. This
is one form of building on a skill. Calling a skill as a routine that starts afresh part-way through an episode is not
tested here, and neither is the answering teacher, who cannot be asked about appended symbols that contradict a history.

Limit: 3,000 episodes of teaching per skill and arm. A skill not taught within it counts as not admitted, and its
demonstrated actions are then a lower bound.

Predictions. With parts: at least 14 of 16 admitted, each at 122/128 or better. Without parts: at least 8 admitted. With
parts needs at most half the demonstrated actions of without, on at least 12 of 16. I expect the gain to grow with the
size of the parts. Stage B decides what is built next and does not decide whether the takeover design is kept.

## Stage B, building on installed skills: result

Sixteen composed skills. The run without parts did not finish within two five-minute invocations on three skills (12-state
parts, 249 to 409 states when flattened); those are counted as not admitted and their effort is unknown.

| Arm | Admitted | Lowest audit, admitted | Prediction |
|---|---|---|---|
| With parts, their actions appended to each observation | 4 of 16 | 126/128 | at least 14, missed |
| Without parts | 2 of 13 finished | 127/128 | at least 8, missed |
| With parts needs at most half the demonstrated actions | 3 of 13 finished, counting only admitted skills | | at least 12, missed |

Stage B fails as designed. Appending the parts' actions to the observations does cut demonstrated actions on 11 of the 13
finished pairs (a ratio between 0.07 and 0.49), but most skills still end near 123 to 125 of 128 on audit and are refused.

The cause is the representation and not the idea. The learner treats each observation with its appended actions as one
opaque symbol, up to 64 of them where there were 4. It cannot express "do what part one does": it has to learn, symbol by
symbol, that the action equals one component of the symbol, and each such transition needs its own demonstration. The
machines it learned have 5 to 32 states where the controller has 2 or 3.

## Stage B again, with a skill as an action: preregistration

One further test, then stop whatever it shows. The learner reads the observations alone, as without parts, but a state's
action may be "the action of part one" or "the action of part two" as well as a fixed action. A demonstrated action is
then explained by every such choice that produces it, and two histories may share a state when some explanation fits both.
Where several explanations remain, a fixed action is preferred. The parts are the same imperfect taught machines, the
skills, gate, audit and limits are as before, and the comparison is with the same figures for the run without parts.

Predictions: at least 14 of 16 admitted at 122/128 or better; demonstrated actions at most half of without parts on at
least 11 of the 13 finished pairs; learned machines of at most 8 states on at least 12 skills.

## Integrated skill as an action: preregistration

The core takeover learner takes helpers, each answering the history so far, and a state's action may be a helper's
answer. The 16 composed skills move into the skills package, with two further draws reserved. Parts are the development
skills taught by demonstration. The arm without parts stops when its machine passes 150 states, so that every skill
finishes within the time limit; its demonstrated actions are then a lower bound.

Predictions: with parts, at least 14 of 16 admitted, each at 122/128 or better; with parts needs at most half the
demonstrated actions of without, on at least 12 of 16. Keep if both hold.

## Choosing parts from the whole library: preregistration

The 16 composed skills again, by demonstration, but the learner is offered all 20 development skills as taught, in a fixed
order, and not only the two each skill is built from. Throwaway script over the integrated code; gate and audit as before.

With three actions, a helper that has nothing to do with a state still gives the demonstrated action about one time in
three, so each visit to a state removes about two thirds of the wrong explanations and 18 extra helpers should cost two
or three more visits per state. The risk is in merging: larger sets of explanations make more pairs of histories look
compatible, and a wrong merge is found only by a later takeover.

Predictions: at least 12 of 16 admitted, each at 122/128 or better; total demonstrated actions at most twice the figure
with two parts (at most 7,140); at most half of teaching without parts on at least 12 of 16. On the admitted skills, the
helpers the machine ends up using are the skill's own two parts on at least 10.

The learner finds the right parts: 13 of the 14 admitted machines use exactly the skill's own two, and the fourteenth
adds one other. The same 14 are admitted as with two parts. The cost is 2.6 times the demonstrated actions where I
predicted at most twice, and it is uneven: 8 skills cost within a fifth of the two-part figure, 4 cost two to five times
it, and one (`2:4:3`) costs six times it. The two skills refused with two parts get worse, keeping 24 and 38 states and
drawing on up to 14 helpers.

So extra helpers are cheap where the learner's merges are right and expensive where they are wrong: each wrong merge
keeps wrong explanations alive until a takeover removes them. The counting bound's logarithmic cost per helper is not
what the merging learner pays on the hard cases. This was a throwaway run over the integrated code; nothing changed in
either repository but this entry.
