# Research diary: 2 November 2026

Incremental finite-log overflow check: two required finite log weights of
`−10³⁰⁸` sum outside binary64 range. The cursor previously reported no
path; native and reference sweeps now report an arithmetic error. A failed
row update leaves the old row available for a subsequent valid update, and
a candidate with no possible suffix does not cause a scoring error. Positive
overflow is also rejected. In seed-250 full-width native forward sweeps,
median times over seven batches of ten calls were 0.009 versus 0.012 ms at
35 words and 0.127 versus 0.172 ms at 143, before and after checks. These
are local microbenchmarks, not end-to-end timings or rounding guarantees.

Prefix-reachability pruning: at position `k`, no reachable counter exceeds
`1 + k·max(A−1, 0)` for maximum arity `A`. Intersecting cursor rows with this
bound avoids scanning impossible states while retaining finite-log checks.
Seed-251 comparisons with fresh decoding after 12 row updates matched for
binary, ternary, and very large unused arities, with and without native
sweeps. Seed-252 full-width forward-sweep medians over seven batches were
0.011 versus 0.008 ms at 35 words, 0.164 versus 0.106 ms at 143, and 2.643
versus 1.678 ms at 575, before and after pruning. These are local native
sweep timings, not end-to-end performance guarantees.

Banded overflow check: the two-word unary-then-leaf sequence with both finite
log weights `−10³⁰⁸` previously returned no result; it now reports arithmetic
overflow. Finite log-tail arithmetic overflow is likewise distinguished from
an absent tail. In-band transitions are restricted to their actual target
interval, and inputs safely inside a per-path magnitude budget use an
unchecked fast path. Seed-253 exhaustive small-band comparisons matched
selected scores and total weights. For seed-254 width-seven passes, medians
over seven batches of ten calls were 0.304 versus 0.229 ms at 35 words and
1.451 versus 0.921 ms at 143 before and after the changes. These Python-path
microbenchmarks are local and do not establish outward-rounded risk bounds.

Native one-shot band pass: the sum and maximum recurrences use two rolling
rows and retain choices for traceback. Seed-255 random bands over binary,
ternary, and very large unused arities agreed with the NumPy fallback, and
seed-253 small bands agreed with enumeration. The existing extreme-overflow
cases remain covered. On seed-256 width-seven inputs, median per-pass times
over seven batches were 0.230 versus 0.014 ms at 35 words, 0.955 versus
0.055 ms at 143, and 3.904 versus 0.326 ms at 575, for NumPy and Elisa
respectively. These include the Python wrapper and are local microbenchmarks,
not end-to-end latency or general numerical-error guarantees.

Banded endpoint check: a band excluding final counter zero cannot contain an
accepted path. Previously the native pass returned no path immediately, but
the NumPy pass could overflow while evaluating an irrelevant intermediate
row. Both now stop at the endpoint check. Seed-258 sparse tied inputs across
2,700 cases, including 1,723 accepted cases, matched between the native and
reference passes. Tests also cover signed 64-bit extreme NumPy band centres,
which are converted before arithmetic to avoid integer overflow. These are
finite comparisons, not a floating-point error bound.

General prefix parser and input check: fractional arity `1.5` was silently
truncated to `1` by the exact and banded decoders; malformed alphabets and
row matrices are now rejected before native calls. The general parser now
uses an iterative stack, including for a 1,500-level prefix, and the Elisa
version matched the reference over all arity sequences of lengths 1–6 with
arities 0–3. Lean verifies that any successful counter run conserves pending
slots and that a complete sequence has `m−1` child edges. Seven-batch local
parser medians for 1,504 words were 0.383 ms in Python versus 0.327 ms in
Elisa, including Python output conversion; these are not end-to-end timings.

Prior alphabet and selection check: with alphabet `(0, 1, 3)`, a selected
symbol `1.5` was truncated to `1` by native format risk, producing zero risk,
while the reference treated it as a different symbol. Risk and prior APIs now
reject noninteger selections before conversion. The prior automaton no longer
restricts its integer arities to 0–2: native and reference computations for
a ternary prefix automaton matched the format decoder's normalizer, maximum
weight, and marginals. A local 575-symbol prior-weight microbenchmark rose
from 0.0070 ms before validation to 0.0146 ms after vectorized type checking;
these are per-call timings, not end-to-end results.

Prior label range check: a prior alphabet containing `10¹⁰⁰` decoded with the
reference implementation but failed native conversion to signed 64-bit.
The prior now relabels out-of-range symbols by dense column index for native
calls and restores original labels on output. A seed-260 four-state prior
matched an explicitly relabelled automaton on selected path, normalizer,
marginals, and selected/alternative partition, with and without native
kernels. Lean proves preservation of a path's prior weight when relabelling
has a left inverse on that path. In a separate ordinary-label microbenchmark,
575-symbol prior-weight calls had medians 0.0147 ms before and 0.0162 ms
after over seven 500-call batches. The separate format decoder still has a
signed 64-bit native alphabet restriction.

Format arity range check: an alphabet containing `10¹⁰⁰` previously failed
native conversion although that arity cannot fit a four-word prefix. For a
fixed `m`-word input, every arity at least `m` is now mapped to the same
unreachable native sentinel; original labels remain in the public interface.
Lean proves every arity in an accepted sequence is less than `m`. Exact,
banded, risk, and incremental results matched their references for an alphabet
with two distinct huge unused arities, including a valid path of log weight
−1000. Seed-261 ordinary three-arity exact-decode medians over seven batches
of 50 calls were 0.0124 versus 0.0130 ms at 35 words and 0.0879 versus
0.0869 ms at 143, before and after; these local timings include noise.

Finite tail support: for `m` words no sequence can differ from a reference
in more than `m` positions, so the tail at `m+1` changes is exactly zero.
Lean verifies the difference-count and empty-tail statements. For uniform
three-arity rows, the former polynomial search selected 1,047 changes at
575 words and 18,126 at 10,000; the finite-support search stops at 576 and
10,001. With the search in Elisa and a Python crossing check for `lgamma`
rounding differences, median complete `band_changes` times over seven
batches fell from 0.176 to 0.072 ms at 575 words and from 3.018 to 1.172 ms
at 10,000. A 2,202-case boundary sweep matched the reference search; these
local timings and comparisons are not end-to-end or outward-rounding proofs.

Tail-reference input check: `band_changes` previously accepted fractional
column indices such as `0.5` and silently converted them to `0`. It now
requires one in-range integer per row, including for both native and reference
execution. Integer arrays are checked with vector operations rather than a
Python per-element loop. For uniform three-column rows, seven-batch median
`band_changes` timings were 0.071 versus 0.038 ms at 575 rows and 1.160 versus
0.562 ms at 10,000 before and after; these local measurements are not
end-to-end performance claims.

Tolerated row-mass excess counterexample: 1,000 identical rows with weights
`(1, 5·10⁻¹³)` pass the existing row tolerance. For one or more changes, the
old `S` bound was about `5.000000000000065·10⁻¹⁰`, below the high-precision
actual tail of about `5.00000000124875·10⁻¹⁰`. Scaling by
`C = Π max(1, row mass)` yields about `5.000000002500057·10⁻¹⁰` and covers
this case. A cursor-update regression checks that the factor is refreshed.
The final scalar scaling inequality is proved in Lean; the full row-scaling
argument remains mathematical rather than mechanized, and floating-point
rounding is not an enclosure. Seven-batch local `band_changes` medians were
0.060 versus 0.061 ms at 575 rows and 0.753 versus 0.695 ms at 10,000 before
and after; these results include noise and are not end-to-end claims.

Band-search experiment: for `S = m/2`, `C = 1`, and slack `10⁻⁴`, the
polynomial bound does not meet the threshold through `m = 100,000`. A single
local native measurement fell from 0.687 ms with a linear scan to 0.001 ms
with a binary search, both returning `m + 1`. These are small, uncalibrated
microbenchmarks, not end-to-end timings. Five hundred seeded cases agreed
with a linear reference, including cases where the bound initially rises.

Full-band tail experiment: when the cursor's band already covers every
reachable state, the outside tail is zero, so its row summary need not be
computed. On seed-268 normalized three-column rows, the native summary alone
had a 4.280 ms median over nine calls at 100,000 rows. This measures the
removed scan in isolation, not an end-to-end cursor speedup.

Tail-summary reuse experiment: a 575-row three-symbol cursor with a narrow
band and no row changes had a 100-call local median normalizer time of
0.0297 ms before summary reuse and 0.0040 ms after. Initialization measured
1.15 and 1.20 ms in single runs. A row update invalidates the summary; the
next certificate recomputes it. These measurements are local and do not
represent the cost of an update or end-to-end training.

Block-summary experiment: for normalized three-column rows and approximately
square-root-sized blocks, seed-270 native kernel medians over 101 calls were
0.0262 ms for a full summary versus 0.0019 ms to refresh and combine blocks
at 575 rows, and 4.241 versus 0.0208 ms at 100,000 rows. The block primitive
was compared with fresh summaries after row updates, including very small
weights and tolerated excess row mass. These are isolated kernel timings.
For a narrow-band 575-row cursor repeatedly updating its middle row, median
normalizer times over 200 calls were 0.0302 ms before block summaries and
0.0086 ms after. Regrouping changes rounding; neither timing is a general
end-to-end performance guarantee.

Block-summary stability: over 3,000 seed-271 single-row updates spanning zero,
subnormal-scale, small, and ordinary weights, the largest difference from a
fresh native scan was 4.0×10⁻¹⁵ in log off-mass and 3.3×10⁻²⁷ in log scale.
The update kernel now visits only the changed block before combining blocks.
The 100,000-row kernel median was 0.0208 ms before and 0.0196 ms after in
separate 101-call local measurements; that small difference may be noise.

Block-size experiment: for seed-272 normalized rows, the existing
`sqrt(m / |A|) + 1` choice gave median update times of 0.0019 ms at
575×3, 0.0088 ms at 575×64, 0.0065 ms at 10,000×3, and 0.0320 ms at
10,000×64. Using `sqrt(m) + 1` instead gave 0.0019, 0.0315, 0.0066, and
0.1304 ms respectively. Each figure is a 61-call local kernel median; the
measurements support accounting for alphabet width but do not establish a
globally optimal block size.

Cursor storage experiment: for a narrow unary-chain band, seed-independent
three-column rows required four tables totalling 668,160, 10,635,264, and
128,192,064 bytes at 143, 575, and 2,000 rows with the previous fixed width.
Sizing by the largest admitted state reduced these to 18,432, 73,728, and
384,192 bytes. Median construction times over nine local runs changed from
0.113, 0.487, and 2.956 ms to 0.107, 0.372, and 1.441 ms respectively.
The bound depends on the largest state reached by the band, so other reference
paths can use substantially more memory. Deferring block initialization was
also tried at 575 rows: first-update median rose from 0.0104 to 0.0331 ms,
with no clear construction gain, so that change was discarded.

Per-row band storage: in a 575-word reference path rising to state 288 and
then falling, with half-width two, storing state `s` at column `s - low[k]`
reduced the four cursor tables from 5,326,848 bytes (289 columns) to 55,296
bytes (3 columns). Median construction time over 21 local runs changed from
0.3737 to 0.3041 ms. The new Elisa kernels and NumPy reference agreed with
independent banded passes through changes and traces. These results describe
one narrow band; full or wide bands retain larger tables.

Packed-row occupancy experiment: for 575 rows over three arities, a full band
allocated 166,464 cells per table while 83,520 state cells were admitted
(49.8% unused). A half-width-two rising band allocated 1,728 cells with 1,724
admitted (0.2% unused). Per-row offsets could remove the unused cells, but
would primarily benefit wider bands in these examples. Independent enumeration
of small bands agreed with packed native and reference cursor scores and traces
across two alphabets, including bands with no accepted paths.

Variable-row cursor storage: seed-275 full-band tables at 143 and 575 rows
used 336,384 and 5,326,848 bytes with fixed-width rows, versus 170,496 and
2,672,640 bytes with row offsets (excluding the offset array). Median native
construction times over nine local runs changed from 0.285 and 3.512 ms to
0.284 and 3.390 ms. At a 143-row narrow band, reference construction medians
over 15 runs were 5.216 ms before and 5.184 ms after reusing row views. These
measurements are local and do not imply a general speedup.

Cursor input layout: seed-276 10,000×64 row-major weights allowed the native
block update to borrow a flat view and took a 0.032 ms median over 31 calls;
Fortran-ordered weights required a copy and took 0.290 ms. In a separate
575×64 cursor test over 101 repeated middle-row updates, row-major private
copies reduced median normalizer-update time from 0.0478 to 0.0377 ms.
Both are local measurements, and the latter includes other cursor work.

Cursor validation experiment: in 3,000 profiled repeated 575-row narrow-band
updates, the certified cursor and underlying split both checked the same row.
After sharing the already-validated row with the split, local medians over
1,000 updates changed from 0.0080 to 0.0060 ms at 143 rows and from 0.0086 to
0.0066 ms at 575. Invalid rows and indices are still rejected before either
cursor copy is changed. These are local normalizer-update timings.

Row-mass reuse experiment: a block update now sums each row's off-reference
mass once and combines it with the reference mass to obtain the row total.
For seed-278 rows, 501-call native kernel medians changed from 0.0027 to
0.0024 ms at 575×3, 0.0115 to 0.0104 ms at 575×64, and 0.0355 to 0.0313 ms
at 10,000×64. High-precision checks include a missing reference, tiny masses,
and an all-zero row. Regrouping changes floating-point rounding, and these
local timings do not measure end-to-end training.

Row-scale accuracy experiment: seed-279 rows with total mass near `1 + 5e-13`
and widths 3, 16, 64, and 256 were compared with 80-digit arithmetic. The
existing block summaries' aggregate log-scale errors were approximately
2.6e-17, 5.9e-17, 1.1e-15, and -1.3e-16. Replacing the last log addition
with an `expm1` formulation did not materially reduce those errors. A separate
compensated linear-sum experiment improved some wide cases but worsened others
and lost excess mass as small as `exp(-40)` when the dominant reference came
last; it was discarded. The block summary is still not outward-rounded.

Anchored off-mass experiment: summing relative to the largest off-reference
log weight with compensation retained the tiny-excess tests. Across 80
seed-280 rows near total mass `1 + 5e-13`, median absolute log-scale error
against 70-digit arithmetic changed from 6.6e-17 to 4.4e-17 at width 64,
from 1.5e-16 to 9.2e-17 at width 256, and from 3.8e-17 to 4.0e-17 at width
16. On separate seed-281 data, 501-call native update medians changed from
0.0346 to 0.0198 ms at 10,000×64. Accuracy can worsen for individual rows;
the calculation remains non-outward-rounded.

Sparse anchored-sum experiment: for 10,000×64 rows, 501-call native block
update medians changed from 0.0064 to 0.0045 ms when only the reference was
finite, from 0.0245 to 0.0213 ms with one finite alternative, and from 0.0217
to 0.0200 ms for dense equal weights. The kernel skips a second scan when
there is no off-reference mass and skips exponentiation for absent weights.
High-precision checks covered repeated transitions among empty, tiny, and
multiple alternatives; local timings are not end-to-end measurements.

Initial-summary reuse: seed-283 native flat and block initialization medians
at 575×64 were 0.740 and 0.198 ms respectively over 31 calls. Reusing the
initial block summary avoids the separate flat scan. In a separate 31-run
cursor construction test, medians changed from 0.3792 to 0.3535 ms at 575×3
and from 15.6688 to 14.9986 ms at 575×64. The cursor and standalone band
selection now use the same native grouping, including at tested threshold
boundaries. Floating-point grouping can differ from the NumPy fallback.

Sweep pruning experiment: for 575 rows and 64 arities, skipping states whose
sum and maximum were both unreachable changed 15-run native construction
medians from 0.720 to 0.598 ms for sparse inputs, but from 15.091 to 16.334 ms
for dense inputs and from 14.521 to 15.239 ms for peaked finite inputs. The
shortcut was discarded because it penalized the latter cases. Profiling the
peaked case still attributed most construction time to the forward and
backward sweeps; tail-summary initialization was a smaller component.

Reachable-envelope experiment: scanning inward from the edges of each sum/max
source row and clipping whole transition ranges avoids visiting states outside
both supports. For 575×64 rows with a fixed band, 21-run local construction
medians changed from 0.870 to 0.636 ms for a sparse unary path, 14.968 to
14.826 ms for dense rows, and 14.413 to 14.114 ms for peaked finite rows.
These are local timings; the latter differences may be noise. The envelope
can contain unreachable holes and does not assume contiguous support.
The same change was also measured on narrow three-arity certified cursors:
51-run construction medians changed from 0.1044 to 0.1024 ms at 143 rows and
from 0.3657 to 0.3656 ms at 575, consistent with no meaningful change there.

Split-scoring envelope experiment: for 575×64 rows, a fixed half-width-63
band, and a dense replacement row, 1,001-call local scoring medians changed
from 0.0107 to 0.0037 ms with sparse prefix/suffix paths, from 0.0365 to
0.0362 ms with dense paths, and from 0.0258 to 0.0257 ms with peaked finite
paths. The latter differences may be noise. The intersection retains holes
inside the support envelopes, and scores and traces were checked against full
decoding for a binary-arity example with such holes.

Trace-junction envelope experiment: for 575×64 rows and a half-width-63 band,
301-call local traceback medians changed from 0.0802 to 0.0662 ms for sparse
paths, from 0.0778 to 0.0762 ms for dense paths, and from 0.0747 to 0.0720 ms
for peaked finite paths. The latter differences may be noise. Tied paths were
checked against the established alphabet-then-state junction order, including
support with holes; these are traceback-only timings.

Traceback ordering experiment: checking for absent weights inside the
reconstruction loop was discarded after 301-call local medians increased from
0.0807 to 0.1094 ms for dense 575×64 rows. For ascending arities, stopping
once later candidates cannot re-enter the state interval instead changed
medians from 0.0810 to 0.0657 ms for sparse paths, 0.0824 to 0.0729 ms for
dense paths, and 0.0719 to 0.0640 ms for peaked paths. Permuted alphabets use
the full scan. At 575×3 with peaked weights, 501-call medians changed from
0.0445 to 0.0388 ms for ascending and 0.0361 to 0.0359 ms for permuted
arities. These are local traceback-only measurements.

Ordered-arity sweep experiment: for a 143-row, 1,024-arity fixed narrow band,
15-run construction medians changed from 1.173 to 0.442 ms in ascending order
and from 1.117 to 1.082 ms with a permutation. At 575×64, medians changed from
14.845 to 14.781 ms ascending and 15.284 to 15.375 ms permuted; at 575×3 they
were essentially unchanged. For the 143×1,024 case, 1,001-call replacement
scoring medians changed from 0.0096 to 0.0066 ms ascending and from 0.0087 to
0.0085 ms permuted. These are local timings; the shortcut never assumes the
caller supplied a sorted alphabet.

Cached arity-order experiment: seed-291 isolated scoring over 1,001 calls
changed from 0.0055 to 0.0050 ms for an ascending 143×1,024 example and from
0.0235 to 0.0204 ms for 143×8,192. Corresponding permuted examples were
0.0079 versus 0.0078 ms and 0.0417 versus 0.0417 ms. The cursor checks its
mapped arities once and keeps that array read-only; the legacy native scoring
entry point still checks ordering itself. Measurements are local and include
Python and input-row handling.

Cached traceback order: over 1,001 local traces at 143 rows and 8,192 arities,
median time changed from 0.0347 to 0.0322 ms for ascending arities; a
permuted alphabet measured 0.5960 versus 0.5938 ms. At 575×3, medians were
0.0369 versus 0.0370 ms ascending and 0.0366 versus 0.0370 ms permuted. The
compatibility entry point still checks ordering itself. The wide permuted case
shows that scanning arbitrary arity orders remains a significant cost; tie
order must be preserved by any future reordering.

Indexed traceback experiment: a stable arity-sorted index lets traceback stop
outside the feasible interval without changing the caller's alphabet. Equal
scores select the smaller original index. For a seed-296 permuted 143×8,192
case, 31-run medians changed from 0.6122 to 0.1265 ms for the first trace
(including index construction), and from 0.5973 to 0.0335 ms for a second
trace. The cached index used 65,536 bytes. Repeated-trace measurements for
seed-295 at 143×1,024 were 0.0895 versus 0.0142 ms permuted, while a 575×3
case was 0.0377 versus 0.0367 ms. These are local traceback measurements;
randomized tied paths matched the previous native implementation exactly.

Trace-index reuse across band rebuilds: the index depends on the alphabet and
row count, neither of which changes when a certified cursor widens its band.
Reusing it avoids a second sort. In a 143×8,192 permuted-alphabet local
rebuild-and-trace experiment, 21-run medians were 12.835 ms before and
12.877 ms after; rebuild work dominated and the timing difference is noise.
The cached index occupied 65,536 bytes in that case.

Log-decoder length-bound experiment: no state requiring more completions than
the remaining word count can reach the final state. Applying that bound to the
Elisa support, forward, and backward loops changed seven-run local medians at
1,024 words from 26.069 to 23.418 ms for dense three-arity rows, and from
6.807 to 5.004 ms for rows supporting only the unary symbol until the final
leaf. At 512 words the corresponding measurements were 6.452 to 6.294 ms
and 1.686 to 1.094 ms. These directly time the log kernel, including its
allocation, with fixed rows and do not characterize end-to-end workloads.

Arity-specific length bound: alternating 13 timed calls to the previous and
revised log kernels on dense fixed rows gave local medians of 10.137 versus
9.383 ms at 128 words and 256 arities, and 72.009 versus 66.607 ms at 256
words and 256 arities. At 512 words and 64 arities they were 174.701 versus
171.693 ms; at 128 words and three arities they were 0.693 versus 0.740 ms.
These kernel timings include allocation and are not end-to-end measurements;
the small-alphabet difference is comparable to local timing variability.

Log-decoder completion-table storage: replacing 64-bit integer flags with
Boolean flags reduced peak resident memory in separate local 2,048-word sparse
kernel runs from 197,148,672 to 167,952,384 bytes, including Python, NumPy,
and all decoder allocations. Alternating eleven calls gave medians of 19.049
and 17.129 ms for that sparse input; at 1,024 dense words the medians were
23.338 and 23.614 ms. These measurements do not imply an end-to-end speedup.

Rolling maximum and backward rows: keeping only two rows for each, while
retaining forward rows and traceback choices, reduced peak resident memory
in separate 2,048-word sparse kernel runs from 167,936,000 to 100,564,992
bytes, including Python and NumPy. Alternating eleven calls gave medians of
17.595 and 13.293 ms on that input, and 22.573 and 21.215 ms for 1,024
dense words. These are local kernel measurements, not end-to-end results.

Rolling rows in the normal-weight decoder: for a completed 2,048-word unary
case, separate runs reduced peak resident memory from 63,078,400 to 46,039,040
bytes, including Python and NumPy. In 21 alternating calls the median kernel
time changed from 9.317 to 7.740 ms for that case; for dense 1,024-word rows
it changed from 3.284 to 3.396 ms. Each timed call was checked to return a
complete normal-weight result rather than the log-fallback sentinel. These
are local measurements, not end-to-end performance claims.

Normal-weight rolling-row clearing: clearing each maximum-score state as it
is consumed removes a separate clearing pass. In 31 alternating calls to
the previous and revised kernels, local medians were 0.488 versus 0.478 ms
for 256 dense words, 3.397 versus 3.329 ms for 1,024 dense words, and 8.158
versus 7.449 ms for 2,048 unary words. All calls completed in normal space;
these timings do not establish an end-to-end speedup.

Log-decoder marginal accumulation: replacing the temporary position-by-arity
table with a scalar accumulator reduced peak resident memory in separate
256-word, 8,192-arity runs from 164,397,056 to 147,603,456 bytes, including
Python, NumPy, the inputs, and the required result. Eleven alternating kernel
calls had medians of 84.822 and 83.253 ms for that case, and 64.178 and
61.132 ms at 256 words and 256 arities. These are local kernel measurements.

Compact log-decoder state rows: using the reachable and completable state
envelope reduced peak resident memory for 2,048 words with arities `(0, 1)`
from 100,663,296 to 29,065,216 bytes in separate local runs, including Python
and NumPy. Eleven alternating kernel calls had medians of 22.342 and 0.096
ms on that input; at 1,024 words with arities `(0, 1, 2)` they were 20.786
and 16.488 ms. The unary case benefits from a linear-size state envelope;
these measurements do not establish an end-to-end speedup.

Impossible arities and state bounds: an arity at least the word count cannot
occur in a complete prefix sequence. Excluding it from the maximum used to
size state ranges prevents an otherwise unary alphabet from appearing highly
branching. For 2,048 words with arities `(0, 1, 2048)`, eleven alternating
local kernel calls had medians of 11.325 versus 0.113 ms in normal space and
8.168 versus 0.104 ms in log space. Separate log-kernel runs had peak resident
memory of 65,060,864 versus 29,376,512 bytes, including Python and NumPy.
The input supported a unary chain and an impossible large arity; these are
not end-to-end measurements.

Avoiding log fallback for impossible arities: with 1,024 dense rows and an
extreme but impossible last-position arity weight, 21 alternating calls to
the public decoder had local medians of 16.569 ms before and 3.248 ms after
skipping that weight. For ordinary dense rows, the corresponding medians
were 3.238 and 3.298 ms. The first case previously retried in log space;
the latter continued in normal space in both versions.

Early-termination mass: a row can heavily favor a leaf even when words remain,
while its viable unary continuation has log weight −690. Discarding premature
termination before normalization and skipping unreachable backward states
let the normal-space kernel complete this case without underflow. For 1,024
words, eleven alternating public-decoder calls had local medians of 3.584 ms
before and 1.068 ms after; a 512-word dense case measured 0.863 and 0.885 ms.
The previous kernel requested a log-space retry for the extreme case. These
are case-specific local measurements.

Weighted-automaton maximum rows: replacing the full maximum-score table with
two rolling rows leaves the predecessor table available for traceback. With
2,048 positions, 1,024 states and one reachable state, separate local kernel
runs reduced peak resident memory from 96,436,224 to 79,822,848 bytes,
including Python and NumPy. Eleven alternating kernel calls had medians of
21.421 versus 18.242 ms. This sparse-state measurement does not establish an
end-to-end speedup or reduce the asymptotic storage of the retained tables.

Weighted-automaton backward rows: emitting each position's marginals during
the reverse pass allows two rolling backward rows. With 2,048 positions,
1,024 states and one reachable state, separate local kernel runs reduced peak
resident memory from 79,577,088 to 63,045,632 bytes, including Python and
NumPy. Eleven alternating kernel calls had medians of 20.261 versus 16.371
ms. Forward sums and traceback choices still require position-by-state
storage; these sparse-state timings do not establish an end-to-end speedup.

Marginal normalization failure: for a one-state, two-symbol weighted prior,
finite log readings on the order of 10³⁰⁸ produced marginal rows `[1, 1]`
in both native and reference decoders; a three-word format example with all
log weights 10³⁰⁷ did likewise. Checking finiteness, nonnegativity and unit
row sums detects these examples but does not certify normalized results.
On this machine, constructing a decoded result with 1,024 three-entry rows
had a 100-call-batch median of 0.0002 ms without validation and 0.0149 ms
with the Elisa check; this isolates the check, not end-to-end decoding.

Partition precision failure: four binary choices with equal log weights
10³⁰⁷ have selected-sequence risk 15/16, but the previous prior partition
returned 1/2 because equal alternative masses merged without changing their
rounded log sum. Detecting that particular lost doubling now rejects the

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.01 | 0.0147 | 1 | 0.0188 | 0.0092 | 0.0100 |

| 0.01 | 0.0147 | 20 | 0.2974 | 0.1734 | 0.1821 |
| 0.05 | 0.0565 | 1 | 0.0606 | 0.0452 | 0.0500 |
