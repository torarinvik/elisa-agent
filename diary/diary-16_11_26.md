## Probe: sokoban by learned consequences and search, now ahead of random presses (development, scratch only)

Carried on from yesterday's probe. Four changes made it work.

The press that ends a puzzle was not being kept as a press, since it comes with a payment, so the player
had never seen what pushing a crate onto the pad does and no search could end. Keeping it mended that.

The four ports are one rule turned four ways. Each port's cases are now tried against every other port's
table under the eight turnings of the grid, and those that agree on every case they share are pooled.
After pooling the four ports hold one table of 486 to 600 cases, each over the two cells either side
along its own way and what the cell began with.

Marking as dead ends the surroundings of a crate met where a puzzle ended unpaid was dropped: four
neighbours do not say whether a crate can still reach the pad, and it set aside pushes that were needed.
With consequences foretold rightly the search does not walk into such a place, because no plan leads out
of it.

A plan once found is followed while the frames go as foretold, and where there is none the player walks
without pushing for thirty presses before it presses at random again.

Two runs of 200 seconds at the first rung, the first 800 presses at random, later half of the puzzles:

| 2 | 37 | 6 of 6 | 9 of 31 |

## The player that arranges tiles, packaged (development)

The arrangement probe of 15 November, put aside then, is now `arranged.py` in `nca-choices` (commit
`d35bd82`). A port that draws a thin mark round a part of the frame and takes it away when pressed again
names a place, and pressing two such ports exchanges what they hold. An arrangement is sought by a
picture shown elsewhere in the frame, by edges that run on into their neighbours (read at four looks,
since tiles may move), or, newly, by each tile being most like the one before it along the places,
forwards or backwards. The way used is the one the task has paid most often. The ways are written by
hand; only the choice among them is learned, from payment.

## A player's own test, made without payment, says where it is worth trying

Chapter 61 bounds an expert by what finding it costs, and 45.7 says a stage with examples of its own is
found alone, so that stages add where otherwise they multiply. Trying every player on every task by
payment is the multiplying case. The question put here: does a player have a test of its own, needing
no payment, that tells the tasks it can work from the rest?

Two were written. The arranging player fits a task where two ports or more mark a place and unmark it.
The grid player fits where every frame has a board and a quarter of forty random presses change a few
of its cells. Each was run once on the seventeen development reasoning tasks and on box pushing and the
maze, first rung, seed 51:

| test | passes on | seconds a task |
|---|---|---|
| arranging: ports that mark places | jigsaw (9), video jigsaw (6), hidden rule (2) | 0.4 to 5.6, and 48 on Go's 361 ports |
| grid: a board, and presses that change a few cells | box pushing (21 of 40), maze (19 of 40) | 2.2 to 5.1 |

The nearest misses of the grid test were signals (6 of 40) and jigsaw (3 of 40). Each test passes on
every task its player has been paid on and on at most one other, and costs seconds where a trial by
payment costs the player's whole share. The thresholds, two ports and a quarter of the presses, were set
after seeing this table, so this is not evidence that they hold elsewhere.

`routed.py` (commit `c814f7f`) now tries a player that states such a test only where the task passes it.
Routed once each at shorter budgets, seed 43: on Go and on who knows what only the three players
without a test were tried; on jigsaw the arranging player was tried and chosen (8 of 8); on box pushing
the grid player was tried and not chosen in 100 seconds, which is less than it needs to learn. The three
older players state no test yet and are still tried everywhere.

## Tasks answered by typing: keys, reading and the answer shown, with recall on top

`typed.py` in `nca-choices` (commit `ed223d9`). Three parts that know nothing of a task, and one small
expert on top of them. A port that ends a puzzle submits, and a port that adds the same glyph twice to a
line is a key. A frame is read as lines cut by empty rows and a line as glyphs cut by empty columns; a
glyph drawn anywhere is matched to a key by its look brought to one size, so the fount and size it is
drawn in do not matter. After an unpaid answer, the line that begins as it did after every such answer
and then differs holds the answer wanted. The expert on top only recalls: the answer is kept under the
lines of the question that few questions have and typed when one is met again.

First rung, 100 seconds, seed 3, run once after the commit; seeds 1 and 2 were used while building:

| task | keys found | puzzles | questions kept | later puzzles answered by recall, paid | random typing paid |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| numberology | 10 | 753 | 54 | 377 of 377 | 0 of 133 |
| algebra | 19 | 729 | 163 | 365 of 365 | 0 of 19 |

Both tasks are mastered at the first rung, and algebra was never looked at: the player written against
numberology ran on it unchanged. But this is recall of answers the task gave, not reasoning. The first
rung of each has few questions (54 and 163), all met within the first tens of puzzles; a question never
met is answered with a random key and is not paid. What it shows is that the three shared parts work
end to end on two tasks, and that such a task gives a learner its answers free of payment, which a
reasoning expert in place of recall can be taught from.

On scramble the same player is paid nothing: the answer there is a line of several words with spaces,
longer than the six glyphs allowed, the lines do not recur, and only 20 of its keys were found.

## A rule found from the answers shown answers questions never met (development)

`ruled.py` in `nca-choices` (commit `6bd1479`) puts an expert that reasons where `typed` had one that
recalls. The first ten keys in the order of their ports are taken as the decimal digits, and a run of
them in a line is a number. A rule is an expression over the numbers of one line, counted from either
end, with sums, differences, products and exact quotients, of at most two signs. The rules of a line
are the smallest that between them give the answers shown for it; the line answered from is the one
whose rules give most of them. That the keys are decimal digits in order is assumed, not found.

Built in scratch against numberology, seeds 5 and 6; algebra was run with it unchanged and has still not
been looked at. 80 seconds, seed 6. A question counts as met for the first time when its line and
numbers have not been seen in the run; it is answered by rule only once rules have been found, which
took five to fifteen guessed puzzles.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| numberology | 1 | 27 of 27 | 1 of 6 | one: the last number and the last difference |
| numberology | 2 | 81 of 90 | 0 of 12 | the same one |
| algebra | 1 | 61 of 61 | 0 of 5 | one: a sum of two numbers |
| algebra | 2 | 123 of 134 | 0 of 15 | two, each of two signs |

## Fold it: answering by where things stand in a picture (development)

Looked at, fold it shows the net of a cube with a letter on each face, asks which face is opposite a

decides the answer is where an answer's letter stands in the net, which it has no way to find.

question names is the one that differs from puzzle to puzzle in a line otherwise the same. That mark and
each answer's are each given a mark of the picture of their own. What is learned, from payment alone, is
a count over steps: how often the answer whose mark stands so many marks down and across from the named
one was paid.

## The shared part of the two experiment players, in the theory and the core

Chapter 65 (experiments that tell rules apart) states what the two players do: narrowing the rules
that could hold by what a test shows keeps the true rule (65.1), one rule left is the true rule
(65.2), and from n rules with b outcomes no fewer than k tests serve in the worst case unless
n ≤ b^k (65.3); three proofs in `NCA.Reliability.Experiments`. The core has `nca.evidence.experiments`
(`fitting`, `splitting`), which names no task. Both players now call it (nca-choices `12fc747`). Seed 105,
100 seconds, second rung, later half paid: hidden wiring 85/85 with 2.7 looks, hidden rule 26/26 with
none or one test; the same as before the change, as it should be.

The bound says why counting the rules matters: with two outcomes and a budget of one test, two rules
can be told apart, and with the two examples given, four; the family the task draws from is that
small, and a learner holding all sixteen tables of two attributes could not stay under the budget.
