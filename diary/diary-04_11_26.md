## Contradiction screening before acquisition (§20.16)

Prediction, before running. A 64-case prefix should expose enough disjoint
contradictory pairs to reject most one-input contracts in the combining
experiment before their 4,096-case fit. The separate false-discard budget is
0.05, spent as η/((k+1)(k+2)); admission retains its own 0.05 budget and fresh
test. With tolerance 0.05, the exact threshold solves
`binom(64,r) 0.05^r ≤ η_k`. Deterministic two-input targets have no
contradictions, so screening should preserve service and evaluated accuracy.
I predict at least a tenfold reduction in unary labels and at least a
twofold reduction in binary labels, fewer fits and lower CPU time.

The benchmark now uses separate random streams for codebooks, teaching cases
and tasks. Both policies therefore receive identical task sequences; teaching
streams diverge when consumption differs. Baselines are rerun rather than
compared directly with the earlier shared-stream numbers. Requests now count
all acquisition attempts, including those on unserved tasks; the former
served-task-only count remains separately named. Report means and sample
standard deviations over seeds 3726–3728. No persistent refusals are stored.

Measured results, mean ± sample standard deviation; labels, requests and fits
are per task, CPU seconds cover each complete 20-task run:

| Policy | Served | Accuracy | Labels | Requests | Fits | CPU seconds |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Unary | 0 | — | 24,582.7 ± 0.2 | 6.00 ± 0.00 | 6.00 ± 0.00 | 0.3202 ± 0.0079 |
| Unary + screen | 0 | — | 384.0 ± 0.0 | 6.00 ± 0.00 | 0.00 ± 0.00 | 0.0140 ± 0.0002 |
| Binary | 1 | 1 | 11,333.7 ± 410.4 | 2.75 ± 0.10 | 2.75 ± 0.10 | 0.2819 ± 0.0201 |
| Binary + screen | 1 | 1 | 1,654.1 ± 7.1 | 2.75 ± 0.10 | 0.35 ± 0.00 | 0.1586 ± 0.0170 |

Both label predictions held: reductions were 64.0× unary and 6.85× binary.
CPU fell 22.8× and 1.78× respectively. Every unary request was screened out;
binary screened 2.40 ± 0.10 requests per task and fitted only the 0.35
admissions. No evaluated deterministic binary target was falsely discarded.
These three seeds do not establish the statistical guarantee: that bound
depends on fresh sampling, deterministic key semantics and sound label
separation. The finite tests also enumerate all six-case samples over two
inputs and two labels against all four predictors, and exactly sum one
eight-case IID false-discard experiment rather than estimating its tail.

Requests are unchanged. The remaining repeated refusals suggest scoped
contract/refusal memory, with distribution and agreement semantics and a
durable spending counter; a statistical refusal must not become an unscoped
permanent ban. Sparse or continuous input keys may yield no collisions, so
this screen gives no universal reduction in label cost.

## Durable append positions under eviction (§43.12)

Prediction, before running. On a workload that appends one record, scans after
the saved cursor, evicts the record and reopens, the previous allocation rule
`max(live positions)+1` repeats position 1. Its model therefore exposes only
the first of 100 or 1,000 admissions. A transactional durable high-water mark
should expose every admission, end at cursor 100 or 1,000 and spend no labels
or expert requests. Measure elapsed and CPU seconds including reopening on
every cycle. This is a correctness experiment, not a claimed speedup. The
legacy comparator is an explicit model, not a separately timed old binary.

Measured results:

| Admissions | Visible after eviction/reopen | Legacy model visible | Last cursor | Elapsed seconds | CPU seconds |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 100 | 100 | 1 | 100 | 0.0575 | 0.0517 |
| 1,000 | 1,000 | 1 | 1,000 | 0.5320 | 0.4736 |

The prediction held exactly; labels and expert requests were zero. This
single-run timing describes overhead, not a speed comparison or a durability
proof. Exhaustive four-operation traces additionally cover duplicate appends
and removal of the newest live record. These observations establish the
cursor prerequisite only: scoped refusal memory and persistent confidence
allocation remain unimplemented. Schema-1 migration requires an external
upper bound on deleted positions, an explicitly trusted input.

## Scoped refusal memory and durable spending (§20.17)

Prediction, before running. Reusing statistically established refusals in the
same versioned context should lower repeated unary requests and labels in the
combining experiment. Screened unary currently uses six new requests and 384
labels per task. I predict at least a twofold reduction in both with refusal
memory; binary should retain service and evaluated accuracy 1 while reducing
labels further. Durable positions include history records and skipped levels,
so admission tests may need more fresh labels than their local-counter
equivalents. CPU may increase because every new reservation is durable; no
speedup is predicted. Reuse the three seeds 3726–3728 and separate task and
sampling streams. Report actual new acquisition requests separately from
cached callback requests; planner callback attempts may remain unchanged.
No failed fit or validation test is persisted as an impossibility claim.

Measured results, mean ± sample standard deviation over the three seeds.
Requests, cache hits and labels are per task; CPU seconds cover 20 tasks:

| Policy | New requests | Cached requests | Labels | CPU seconds | Served | Accuracy |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Unary + screen | 6.00 ± 0.00 | 0 | 384.0 ± 0.0 | 0.01386 ± 0.00005 | 0 | — |
| Unary + memory | 1.80 ± 0.00 | 4.20 ± 0.00 | 115.2 ± 0.0 | 0.01835 ± 0.00024 | 0 | — |
| Binary + screen | 2.75 ± 0.10 | 0 | 1,654.1 ± 7.1 | 0.15818 ± 0.02483 | 1 | 1 |
| Binary + memory | 2.08 ± 0.10 | 0.67 ± 0.08 | 1,617.8 ± 6.7 | 0.17397 ± 0.01723 | 1 | 1 |

The unary prediction held: new attempts and labels fell 3.33×. Binary labels
fell only 2.2%; its remaining cost is mostly training and certifying admitted
experts, and skipped durable positions make certification more conservative.
No accuracy or service loss was observed. CPU increased 32% unary and 10%
binary, consistent with the predicted possibility of durable-write overhead;
these small timings are not a general throughput result. Timings exclude
initial database creation but include task-time durable writes. Planner callback
attempts are unchanged: the history prevents repeat sampling and fitting after
the callback, rather than removing those candidates from type-driven search.

Restart tests reuse a stored refusal with zero new labels, confirm that a
changed context retries at a smaller spending level, and interrupt sampling
after reservation to verify that reopening cannot reuse its level. Bounded
index misses and eviction trigger new reservations; neither resets the clock.
The remaining learning bottleneck is admitted experts' large fixed training
budget. Semantic candidate selection and planner-level avoidance of known
refusals remain separate open mechanisms.

## Staged acquisition instead of a fixed training size (§20.18)

Prediction, before running. Declare training stages 64, 128, 256, 512, 1,024,
2,048 and 4,096, splitting each request's admission level over all seven
stages. Reuse failed tests as training only for later candidates. On the
combining experiment, a table trained on 1,024 examples should miss about
`(255/256)^1024 ≈ 1.8%` of value pairs. It will often fail its zero-error test
of roughly 150–250 fresh cases, but at 2,048 examples it should usually pass.
I predict all tasks remain served, evaluated error below the declared 0.05
tolerance, at least a 25% reduction in labels relative to fixed-budget binary
with memory, and more fits per task. CPU and total examples processed across
fits may increase, so measure both rather than claiming a universal saving.
Rerun seeds 3726–3728 with the same task streams. Teaching streams may diverge
because tests consume different amounts. The maximum training size remains
4,096, and every candidate test gets fresh data and its own confidence share.

Measured results, mean ± sample standard deviation over three seeds; requests,
labels, fits and examples processed by fitters are per task. CPU seconds cover
the 20-task run, excluding initial database creation:

| Policy | Labels | Fits | Fitted examples | New requests | CPU seconds | Served | Accuracy |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Fixed + memory | 1,617.8 ± 6.7 | 0.35 ± 0.00 | 1,433.6 ± 0.0 | 2.08 ± 0.10 | 0.17569 ± 0.01761 | 1 | 1 |
| Staged + memory | 440.0 ± 26.9 | 1.00 ± 0.13 | 461.9 ± 57.4 | 2.08 ± 0.10 | 0.16434 ± 0.02500 | 1 | 0.99681 ± 0.00553 |

The label prediction held: 72.8% fewer labels (3.68× reduction), all tasks
served and evaluated error below 0.05. Accuracy decreased from 1 to 0.99681;
certification permits nonzero error, so this is an observed tradeoff, not
perfect-answer preservation. Fits increased 2.86×, but total examples fed to
fitters fell 67.8%, and mean CPU fell 6.5%. The overlapping timing variation
does not establish a reliable speed improvement.

A diagnostic trace on seed 3726 explains why the result beat the prediction:
the two new two-input experts passed at 2,048 training cases, while five
acquired one-input representation connectors passed at 64 or 128. The fixed
policy had trained all of those on 4,096 cases. This is evidence for adapting
training to each connector, rather than assuming every acquisition has the
same learning difficulty. It does not establish efficiency for other fitters.
Requests and cached callbacks stayed unchanged. The next limitation is that
stage sizes and candidate selection are still supplied policies, not learned
from capability demand or observed learning curves.

## Learned acquisition priorities (§20.19)

Prediction, before running. Compare fixed gap ordering with a learned priority
in the combining experiment, using fresh installed libraries for each of the
20 tasks (independent deployments). Neither policy reuses acquired experts or
refusal memory across those deployments; only the learned ranking policy
transfers its success statistics. Both retain the same base connectors and
staged training/certification. This isolates ranking from reuse, rather than
claiming a replacement for an installation that retains its experts.

The benchmark policy estimates success probability with a Beta(1,1) prior,
grouping observations by input count and optimizing unit request cost. No
arity preference is supplied: initial estimates tie. After one failed unary
request and one successful binary request it should prefer binary gaps in
this particular task family. I predict requests fall from seven per task to
about 1.05, labels by at least 10%, every task is served, and evaluated error
remains below 0.05. Mean CPU may fall from fewer repeated gap searches, but
measure rather than assume that. Scores are learned estimates, not a proved
calibrated model, and other task families may reverse the correlation.

Measured results, mean ± sample standard deviation over seeds 3726–3728;
requests and labels are per task, CPU seconds cover all 20 deployments:

| Independent-deployment policy | Requests | Labels | CPU seconds | Served | Accuracy |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Fixed ordering | 7.00 ± 0.00 | 2,677.2 ± 106.6 | 0.14970 ± 0.00312 | 1 | 0.99922 ± 0.00103 |
| Learned ordering | 1.05 ± 0.00 | 2,141.2 ± 51.2 | 0.08272 ± 0.00111 | 1 | 0.99870 ± 0.00011 |

The predictions held: 85% fewer requests, 20.0% fewer labels, every task
served and evaluated error below 0.05. Mean CPU was 44.7% lower. The policy
learned the useful ordering from one failure and one success; the core
contains neither its feature choice nor an arity preference. Accuracy shifted
slightly because consuming fewer requests and cases changes both teaching
streams and confidence levels; both policies retain fresh certification.

This is a controlled positive example for ranking, not evidence that input
count predicts learnability generally. The policy optimizes unit request
cost; varying label/compute costs or a changed task distribution can make its
ordering poor. The exchange theorem assumes true fixed probabilities and
costs, not these estimates. Calibrated contextual predictions, distribution
change detection and risk-targeted acquisition remain open.

## Voting bounds under dependent failures (§31.8)

Prediction, before running. Evaluate depths 2 and 8, route counts 3 and 7,
seeds 3716–3718, both separately sampled and common-case training. Common-case
training reuses each depth's latent training cases across all lanes, so the
fitted routes should miss the same inputs despite distinct implementations.
Their independent-product bound should fail in at least one correlated cell.
The marginal-only bound should cover the exact uniform-input vote failure in
every cell. All 64 possible inputs are evaluated, so lane frequencies here
are exact marginals of that finite distribution, not estimates from a test
subset. There is no claim of coverage on other distributions.

No additional labels or fitting requests are needed for the marginal-only
calculation. Record total CPU, 256×7×depth training pairs and 7×depth fits.
The cost of conservatism is expected: with identical errors of rate p the
new equal-risk bound is kp/ceil(k/2), capped at one, rather than an amplified
independent bound. Exact rational evaluation followed by upward conversion
also prevents numerical understatement for the supplied float marginals.

abstention. The two bounds use each fitted collection's exact marginals over
the 64-input uniform distribution:

The predictions held. The dependence-safe bound was violated in 0/24 runs;
the independent bound was violated in 12/12 common-case runs and 4/12
separate-case runs. Separate training datasets do not ensure independent
errors for the fixed fitted models. Common-case failure rates were identical
for three and seven voters: extra correlated routes did not improve accuracy.
The conservative bounds increased with that extra redundancy rather than
claiming amplification. This is not evidence of additional capability, but
does prevent an unsupported reliability claim.

Depth-2 runs used 3,584 training pairs and 14 fits; depth-8 runs used 14,336
pairs and 56 fits. The benchmark fits all seven available lanes even when
only three vote. The new bound requires no additional labels or fitting
requests. CPU measurements cover fitting, planning, exact marginal enumeration,
voting and both bound calculations; they are single-run-per-seed overhead
measurements, not a throughput comparison. The next reliability gap is
acquiring or certifying an ensemble against a target error using actual joint
evidence, rather than assuming independence from how experts are stored.

## Acquire and certify an actual vote (§31.9)

Prediction, before running. At depth 8 and target error 0.02, compare acquiring
nine lanes before one fresh vote test with testing growing votes at sizes
1, 3, 5, 7 and 9. Fit only requested lanes. Use seeds 3716–3718 with separate
random streams for fitting and certification, and both separate-case and
common-case training. Each search has confidence 0.05, split over its declared
test counts. Validation labels are additional to the 256×depth labels for
each acquired lane; evaluate accepted votes on all 64 inputs afterward.

I predict at least two of three separate-case growing searches certify, at
least one stops before nine lanes, and no common-case search certifies an
ensemble above the target. No false certificate is expected in these seeds.
Repeated tests can increase validation cost and false refusal, so record
acceptance as well as route requests, fit requests, training labels,
certification labels and CPU. Fixed and staged fitting use the same lane
factory and seed: their first n lanes match, independently of test stopping.

| Common cases | Growing | 0/3 | 9.00 | 72.00 | 18,432 | 25.0 | 0.01102 |

Predictions held. Every returned certificate had zero error on the complete
64-input distribution; there were no observed certificates above target.
Growing separate-case searches used 29.6% fewer route/fit requests and training
examples, and 29.1% fewer total labels including validation (13,179 against
18,576). Mean CPU was 34.2% lower in these three runs; this is not a general
throughput guarantee. One of three searches was refused by each policy, so
admission quality must not be confused with universal service.

With common-case errors, neither policy returned a certificate. Growing used
more validation labels and 13.8% more mean CPU; extra routes did not repair
the shared blind spot. A proposal policy that changes the source of capability,
rather than merely adding similarly trained lanes, remains necessary there.
The certification mechanism itself makes no claim of independence or of
learning such a policy. The next gap is choosing new routes to address
observed joint failures without contaminating their future certification cases.
