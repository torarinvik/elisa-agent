# 60. A situation completed from itself

Every learner of §52 to §59 is taught: it is shown which completion of a situation was right, many times, and
finds what the right ones have in common. Some situations say how they are to be completed. A matrix shows its
rule in every row but the last. This chapter reads the rule off the situation in hand, with nothing taught.

## 60.1 Model

A situation holds **lines** of slots that stand alike, each line the same number of slots. All lines but the
last are **whole**; the last lacks its last slot, and each candidate fills it. That the lines are these, and
that what governs the situation runs along them, is given by whoever names the slots. Which of two ways
the lines run can be found (60.6).

A **rule** is a set of lines, those that follow it. A situation follows a rule when all its lines do once the
right candidate is in place. Nothing says which rule a situation follows, and the rules are not listed.

A **reading** of a line is a function of its values (§55): whether two are the same, a step, the step between
two steps, what a step holds beyond a value (55.15), what the line's values are between them whatever their
order, and for sets how one is made of two or turned (55.19, 55.20). A rule **keeps** a reading when the
reading is the same on every line that follows the rule.

## 60.2 What the whole lines agree on

Take the readings on which all the whole lines agree, and choose the candidate whose line keeps the most of
them.

**Proposition 60.1 (the right candidate keeps what the rule keeps).** If a situation follows a rule, every
reading the rule keeps is agreed on by the whole lines and kept by the right candidate's line.

**Proposition 60.2 (a wrong candidate breaks what turns on the last slot).** Let a reading be one to one in
the last slot: for fixed earlier values, different last values read differently. If the right candidate keeps
it, every candidate that fills the last slot otherwise breaks it. A step to the last slot, the step between the
last two steps and the excess of 55.15 are such readings; whether two slots are the same is not.

So where the rule keeps a reading that is one to one in the last slot, the right candidate is ahead of every
other on that reading. It is not thereby ahead in all: the whole lines can agree, by the values they happen to
hold, on a reading the rule does not keep, and a wrong candidate can keep that one.

**Proposition 60.3 (two lines can leave it open).** The lines `(5, 1, 3)` and `(4, 1, 2)` agree that the last
is the first less the second less one, and agree that it is the first plus the second less three. The line
`(4, 2, ·)` is completed by 1 under the first and by 3 under the second. So no way of choosing from the
situation alone is right on every situation: which of two readings the makers of a kind of situation keep is
knowledge of that kind, and is what teaching gives.

The fewer the whole lines, the more they agree on by chance: with one whole line every reading is agreed on.
With two, a reading with `v` values equally likely is agreed on by chance once in `v`.

## 60.3 What turns on the last slot is counted first

Counting every agreed reading alike lets many that say little outvote one that says all. Whether two slots
are the same is agreed on by most pairs of lines and kept by most candidates; the right candidate breaks it
whenever the last line happens to repeat a value the lines above do not.

The lines `(1, 2, 3)` and `(2, 3, 5)` agree that the last is the sum of the first two, that it is not the
first and that it is not the second. The line `(0, 0, ·)` is completed by 0 under the sum. Counting alike, 0
keeps one of the three and 7 keeps two.

So the readings are grouped by how far they fix the last slot, and a candidate that keeps more of an earlier
group is ahead whatever it keeps of the later ones: first the readings one to one in the last slot, then those
one to one in something read of it, such as how many a set holds, then the others. A reading of yes or no
that reads no is of the last group: that the last is not made of the first two in some way leaves it open.
What a line holds between its slots is of the first group where the last line's earlier slots hold all of it
but one, for then one value alone completes it, and of the last group otherwise.

**Proposition 60.4 (what turns on the last slot is kept by the right candidate alone).** Let the whole lines
agree on one or more readings that are one to one in the last slot, each kept by the rule. The right
candidate keeps them all and every other candidate none. So counting them first chooses the right candidate
whatever the lines agree on by chance among the other readings.

What stays open is 60.3 within the first group: the whole lines can agree by chance on a reading that is one
to one in the last slot, and a wrong candidate can keep that one.

## 60.4 What this is and is not

Choosing so needs no judgement of any earlier situation. It needs the lines, the readings, and whatever makes
the values: the same reader of pictures as the taught learner, whose shapes and sizes are found from pictures
with no answer shown (55.17, 55.18). It does not learn: what it cannot read off one situation it cannot read
off a thousand. The taught learner of §54 and §57 does, and is not bound to lines. The two meet in 60.3: what
a situation leaves open, experience of its kind closes.

## 60.5 What a situation leaves open, experience closes

Two choosers are heard in turn. The first is that of 60.2 and 60.3, which needs nothing taught. Among the
candidates it holds level, and only among them, the second decides: a learner taught on situations of the
kind (§57). The second never overrules the first.

**Proposition 60.5 (the later chooser decides only where the earlier is level).** Let the first chooser hold
no candidate ahead of the right one, and let the second hold the right one ahead of every other candidate the
first holds level with it. Then the two in turn choose the right candidate. In particular they are right
wherever the first alone is, whatever the second was taught, and wrong only where the first holds a wrong
candidate ahead, or holds one level and the second prefers it.

So the learner is asked only what 60.3 says no situation can tell: which of the completions its rows leave
level the makers of the kind keep. It need not learn the rules the rows already show.

## 60.6 Which way the lines run

That the rule runs along the rows was given in 60.1. It can be found from situations of the kind with no
answer. Read of each only the lines that are whole whichever way the lines run. Along the way the rule runs,
those lines agree on every reading the rule keeps (60.1). Across it they need not.

**Proposition 60.6 (across the rule the lines need not agree).** In the rows `(1, 2, 3)`, `(2, 3, 5)`,
`(4, 1, ·)` the last of a row is the sum of the first two, and the two whole rows agree on it. The two whole
columns `(1, 2, 4)` and `(2, 3, 1)` agree on none of the four readings of 60.3 that are one to one in the
last slot.

So for each attribute of each part it is counted, each way, whether the whole lines agree on some reading
that fixes a line's last slot, and the way is taken along which more do. How many readings agree is not
counted: a rule that keeps a row's slots alike makes the columns the same line, and lines that are the same
agree on every reading, more than the rows themselves do. Nor does a rule across the lines always go unseen:
rows that are alike, rows that step alike and rows that hold the same values each give columns that agree.
What tells the ways apart is the rules that do not, of which a sum is one; a kind of situation with no such
rule is completed alike either way.

## 60.7 One line, completed from its own steps

A situation of one line has no whole lines to agree. But the steps between its neighbours are a line, one
shorter, and they stand alike: each is a line of 60.1 for the one after. If they are all the same, the line is
completed by that step. If not, the steps are themselves a line to be completed, in the same way, and the
line is completed by the step so found. A step is taken in one of several **ways**, each with what leads from
one value to the next and the value a step leads to; what is added is one, what is multiplied by another.

Of the lines of steps so reached, each by some ways in turn, the shallowest whose steps all agree, two or
more of them, completes the line: two steps that agree were not made to. Where none agrees, one way is
followed down to a single step, on which, as with one whole line in 60.2, everything is agreed.

**Proposition 60.7 (a line is completed by completing its steps).** Let each term of a line be the one before
with a step added. If what is said to follow the steps is the step that does follow, then the last term with
that added is the term that follows the line. So where the steps of the steps of a line, taken `d` times, are
all the same, the line is completed rightly by `d` such completions: a line whose terms are a polynomial of
degree `d` in their place is completed from `d + 1` of its terms.

Nothing here is taught, and nothing says how deep the rule of a line lies: it is read off the line. What a
line of few terms cannot show it does not find: a rule that reaches back three terms is not shown by four.

## 60.8 A line alike at a stride, and steps taken by their place

Steps that are all the same are alike at a stride of one. A line may be alike at a longer stride: each value
the same as the one `k` before it. Such a line is completed by its value `k` back, and it is shown to be so
once one value past the first `k` is seen: `k + 1` values. A line of steps reached by stepping `d` times and
alike at stride `k` is so shown by `d + k + 1` terms of the line above all. Of the lines of steps that are
alike at some stride, the one shown by the fewest terms completes the line, and among such the one of the
shortest stride; so 60.7 is the case of stride one, and nothing it completed is completed otherwise unless a
line as short is alike where 60.7 found none.

A way of stepping may also take a step by its **place** in its line: what is added, over how many steps have
been taken. The steps 2, 6, 6, 12 agree at no stride; by their places they are 2, 3, 2, 3.

**Proposition 60.8 (a line alike at a stride is completed from a stride back).** Let each term of a line be the
one before with a step added, and let the steps be alike at stride `k`. Then the term after place `p + k` is
that term with the step at place `p` added. If instead each step is its place times a rate, and the rates are
alike at stride `k`, the term after place `p + k` is that term with the rate at place `p` times the place
`p + k + 1` added.

A stride is not handed over, nor that places matter: both are read off the line, as depth is in 60.7. The
price is that a short line can be alike at a stride by chance where its rule lies deeper than it shows.

## 60.9 Which slots are a line

So far a situation came cut into lines, and 60.6 chose between two cuts handed over. Nothing in 60.1 needs
that: the slots may be handed over as a bare set with one of them asked for, and the cut found. A **cut** of
`n k` slots is `n` lines of `k` slots in order, the asked slot last in its line. Lines exchanged whole give
the same cut, so there are `(n k − 1)! / (n − 1)!` cuts, and `(n k − 1)! / ((n − 1)! (k − 1)!)` choices of
the whole lines alone: 20,160 and 10,080 for nine slots in threes.

Over situations of one kind with no answer, the whole lines of a cut are scored as in 60.6, by how many
attributes they agree on in some reading that fixes a line's last slot. That score cannot see the asked line:
the slots left over stand before the asked slot in an order nothing shown says. And it cannot tell a cut
from the same cut with every whole line read backwards:

**Proposition 60.9 (lines read backwards agree where they agreed).** If the steps of two lines of three are
the same, so are the steps of the two read backwards; and if the last of each is the first plus the one
between, then read backwards the last of each is the first less the one between.

What decides both is the completions offered, still with none marked right: one of them is the rule's, so
under the rule's cut some completion keeps, in every attribute, what the whole lines agree on, and under
another cut often none does. A cut is scored by how many attributes its best completion keeps so, summed over
the situations, among the cuts whose whole lines scored highest.

Nothing of where a slot stands is used: a slot is a name. What is given is that the lines are alike in
length, and how long.
